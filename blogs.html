Design

Before the first
part is made.

A useful design starts with the question it needs to answer.

Before opening a CAD model, write down the purpose of the part. What does it connect to? What loads might it experience? How will it be manufactured, assembled, and inspected?

These questions turn a shape into an engineering decision. Start with a sketch and a short list of constraints. Then use the model to explore alternatives rather than treating the first version as the finished answer.

Look beyond the individual part.

A component belongs to a system. Clearances, mounting points, available tools, and access for maintenance matter alongside geometry. Reviewing a design with someone from another discipline can reveal questions that were easy to overlook.

Process

Build. Test.
Ask better questions.

A test is most useful when you know what you want to learn.

Choose a specific question before running a test. Define what you will observe, what conditions you will use, and what result would lead you to change the design. Record the setup so the next test can be compared fairly.

When a result differs from an expectation, inspect the assumptions as well as the component. A loose connection, a measurement error, or a change in the test conditions can alter what the result means.

Make the next version deliberate.

Document the observation, propose an explanation, and change one relevant variable where practical. Iteration becomes more valuable when the team can explain why each version is different.

Teamwork

Leave a trail for
the next engineer.

The reasoning behind a decision is part of the work.

A model or drawing shows what was made. A short design note explains why. Keep the requirements, alternatives considered, important dimensions, and unresolved questions close to the project files.

Clear file names and revision notes make it easier to collaborate. Record what changed and why, and make sure the drawing and model refer to the same version before anyone begins manufacturing.

Keep notes useful, not elaborate.

A few specific sentences, an annotated sketch, and a test observation are often more helpful than a long report written after the details have been forgotten. Write for the person who will pick up the work next.
